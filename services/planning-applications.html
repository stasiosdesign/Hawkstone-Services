Routes

The planning
routes we prepare

Choosing the wrong route is one of the most common reasons an application stalls at validation. These are the routes we deal with most often, and what each is for.

Householder planning application

Extensions, alterations, outbuildings and other works to a single house within its garden. Most are determined within eight weeks of validation.

Full planning application

New dwellings, replacement dwellings, changes of use and anything beyond householder scope. Every detail of the scheme is decided at once.

Prior approval

Permitted development that still needs the council to sign off specific matters, most often Class Q barn conversions.

Outline and reserved matters

Outline planning permission establishes the principle of building on a plot before the detail is designed; reserved matters follows with the design. Useful when you want to test or sell a site.

Amendments and discharge of conditions

Non-material and minor material amendments to an approval, and the condition submissions that must be cleared before work starts on site.

Retrospective applications & lawful development certificates

For work already carried out, or to confirm formally that a proposal does not need permission.

Site appraisal before you pay for drawings

The worry we hear most often is spending money on surveys and drawings and then being refused. The answer is to establish what is likely to be acceptable before the design starts. Our site appraisal looks at the planning history of the property, the local plan policies that apply, designations such as Green Belt, conservation areas and flood zones, neighbour relationships, access, and anything else a case officer will raise. You receive a written view of the opportunities, the risks and the route we would recommend.

Where a proposal is finely balanced, we prepare a pre-application enquiry to the council. Pre-application advice is not binding, but on a new dwelling, a Green Belt site or a sensitive plot it usually shows the officer’s direction of travel before the full fee and a full set of drawings are committed. On a straightforward extension it is rarely worth the time or the council’s charge, and we will say so.

Refused planning permission? What we do next

A refusal is rarely the end of a project. The decision notice lists the reasons, and each reason is either something the design can answer or something the policy will not allow. We read the notice and the officer’s report, tell you which it is, and set out the realistic options.

FAQs

Questions we’re asked about
planning applications

  • Do I need planning permission for my project?

    Not always. Many extensions, outbuildings and alterations fall within permitted development, though the limits are tighter in conservation areas, on listed buildings and where earlier rights have been removed by condition. Anything that creates a new dwelling, changes the use of a building or exceeds the permitted limits needs an application. Our site appraisal answers this for your specific property, and a lawful development certificate can confirm it formally.

  • Do I need an architect for planning permission?

    Legally, no. Anyone can submit an application. In practice, the quality of the design and the drawings is what the case officer, the parish council and your neighbours respond to, and a scheme that has been designed rather than simply drawn is far more likely to be approved and to be worth building afterwards. An architect-led application also carries straight through into technical design and construction without being redrawn.

  • What drawings are required for a planning application?

    A location plan at 1:1250, a block or site plan at 1:500 or 1:200, existing and proposed floor plans, elevations and sections, usually at 1:100 or 1:50, and a design and access statement for most applications beyond householder scope. Depending on the site, the council may also validate against a heritage statement, ecology survey, tree survey, flood risk assessment or transport statement. We prepare the drawings and commission the reports.

  • Householder or full planning permission: which do I need?

    Householder covers works to an existing single house within its garden: extensions, loft conversions, outbuildings and alterations. Anything that creates a separate dwelling, changes the use of a building, or affects a flat or a subdivided house needs a full application. The distinction changes the fee, the forms and the determination period, and getting it wrong delays validation. Our guide to the types of planning permission sets out the full list.

  • Is pre-application advice worth it?

    On a new house, a Green Belt or open countryside site, or anywhere the answer is not obvious, usually yes: it surfaces objections early and shows the officer’s likely position. On a standard extension it rarely adds anything, and the council’s fee and turnaround are better spent on the application itself. We advise case by case.

  • Can you tell me whether my project is likely to get permission?

    We can give you a well-founded view, which is what the site appraisal is for. No one can guarantee an approval, but a proposal built around the policies that apply, with the likely objections designed out, has a strong chance of success. If we think a site will not support what you have in mind, we say so before you spend money on drawings.

  • How much does a planning application cost?

    There are two costs. The council’s application fee is set nationally and depends on the type of application. The larger cost is the design and submission work: survey, drawings, statements and any specialist reports the site needs. We quote a fixed fee once the site appraisal has established the route and the scope. Our guide to planning permission costs sets out typical figures.

  • How long does planning permission take?

    Eight weeks from validation for householder and most minor applications, thirteen weeks for major ones, and longer where an extension of time is agreed with the council. Add the design period before submission, which depends on the complexity of the scheme and whether surveys are needed. Our guide to planning timescales explains what to expect at each stage.

  • Will neighbour objections affect my application?

    Only material ones. Loss of light, overlooking, overbearing scale, noise and parking are planning matters and will be weighed. Loss of a view, property values and disruption during construction are not. A well-designed scheme anticipates the material points, and we respond formally to any objections raised during the consultation period.

  • What happens if planning permission is refused?

    You can amend and resubmit, appeal to the Planning Inspectorate within the time limit, or rethink the proposal. Which is right depends on the reasons given in the decision notice, and we set out the options above under refused planning permission.
