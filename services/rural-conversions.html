Types of barns
we work with

  • Stable block.

    Usually a single-storey brick or stone range with a row of stalls and doors along one side, which lends itself to a linear plan of connected rooms.

  • Steel portal frame.

    A sound portal frame is often a simpler starting point than a derelict stone barn.

  • Dutch barn.

    An open-sided hay barn with a curved or pitched roof on a steel or timber frame: the walls and insulation are new, while the frame and roof set the character.

  • Farm outbuilding.

    Cart sheds, granaries, cowsheds and workshops, often grouped around a farmyard, whose scale and layout shape what a conversion can become.

  • Stone threshing barn.

    A tall traditional barn with thick masonry walls and large opposing doors, whose open volume and few openings call for care with light and structure.

Barn conversion
planning permission:
Class Q or full planning

Almost every project starts with the same question: do I need planning permission for a barn conversion? There are two routes, and choosing the right one early saves months.

01

Class Q prior approval

Class Q permitted development allows certain agricultural buildings to be converted to homes without a full planning application. We assess your building against the criteria, prepare the prior approval application, and design within its limits. It is faster and gives councils less discretion, but the eligibility rules are strict. Our Class Q guide explains them.

02

Full planning permission

Where a building doesn’t qualify for Class Q, or where a better scheme is possible than Class Q allows, we prepare a full planning application, making the case for conversion on its merits. Many of our strongest projects went this way. Green Belt and countryside policy add constraints we deal with routinely; see our Green Belt guide.

03

Class R

Class R is the commercial counterpart to Class Q: it lets an agricultural building become offices, shops, a café, storage or a hotel, but not a home. Up to 150 m² needs only notice to the council; larger schemes, up to 1,000 m², need prior approval on transport, noise, contamination and flood risk. It covers the change of use, not building works, and excludes listed buildings.

Our approach

Feasibility before design

The most expensive mistake in this type of project is designing a scheme the building can’t support or the council won’t approve. So we start with feasibility:

Structural condition

01

Can the walls, frame and foundations take conversion? Planning law expects a genuine conversion, not a new build barn conversion in disguise — if the building would effectively need rebuilding, the route and the risks change, and you should know that before committing to design fees.

Surveys

02

A measured survey to design from, a structural engineer’s appraisal, and where relevant an ecology and bat survey or contamination assessment. We tell you which are actually needed for your building and sequence them so nothing is paid for twice.

Access and services

03

Highways access, water, drainage and power — unglamorous, but they decide budgets and sometimes planning outcomes.

Design that earns the building its second life

The best conversions still read as agricultural buildings. We keep the volume, the honest structure, the long uninterrupted roofs, and solve what conversion demands: natural light in deep plans, insulation that doesn't suffocate the fabric, and rooms that work as a home rather than a compromise.

The result can be restrained or a fully contemporary barn conversion. Several of our projects sit at the luxury barn conversion end of the spectrum. Where the planning route allows, extending a barn conversion can rescue a layout the original footprint never could, and a well-judged barn conversion extension often makes the difference between workable and genuinely good.

FAQs

Questions Before You Start

  • Do I need planning permission for a barn conversion?

    You need either Class Q prior approval or full planning permission — every conversion needs one of the two. Which applies depends on the building’s history and condition; establishing that is the first thing we do.

  • Does my barn qualify for Class Q permitted development?

    Agricultural use on the qualifying date, a structure genuinely capable of conversion, and the size and number limits of the Class. The detailed rules are in our Class Q guide , or we assess your building directly at consultation.

  • What happens if my barn doesn’t qualify for Class Q?

    The route becomes a full planning application. That is often an opportunity, not a setback — full planning can permit a better scheme than Class Q’s limits allow.

  • Can a modern steel-framed or block agricultural building be converted?

    Frequently, yes. Class Q does not require a traditional building, and a sound portal frame is a good starting structure. Condition, not age or looks, is what matters.

  • Is it a conversion or a rebuild?

    If the existing structure can’t substantially carry the works, planning treats the project as a new build, and the rules change entirely. Our structural appraisal answers this before you commit.

  • What surveys are needed for a barn conversion?

    Typically a measured survey and a structural appraisal; often an ecology or bat survey; occasionally contamination or drainage assessments. We specify only what your building and council actually require.

  • How long does a barn conversion take?

    As a guide: feasibility in weeks; Class Q prior approval around two months in the system, full planning longer; technical design and construction typically put a whole project at eighteen months to two and a half years from first conversation to moving in.

  • Can I extend a barn as part of the conversion?

    Not under Class Q, beyond its tight limits — but under full planning, yes, where the design justifies it. If an extension matters to you, it can shape which route we recommend.

  • Do I pay VAT on a barn conversion?

    Much conversion work qualifies for the reduced 5% VAT rate rather than 20%. It’s a significant saving that’s often missed; we flag it at feasibility stage so your budget reflects it.
