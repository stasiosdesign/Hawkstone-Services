Architectural Design
for Listed Buildings

Heritage-led design for listed buildings, locally listed properties and homes in conservation areas, with the Listed Building Consent and planning applications to support it. We work with private clients across Derbyshire, Staffordshire and Cheshire.

The buildings we
work with

Protection comes in several forms, and the type applying to your property changes what you need to apply for and what evidence has to sit behind it. These are the situations we work in most often.
01
Listed buildings

Grade I, Grade II* and Grade II properties, where the listing covers the whole building inside and out, along with fixtures and often structures within the curtilage. Almost all of our listed work is Grade II.
02
Homes in
conservation areas

Undesignated houses within a designated area, where the character of the wider street and the setting carry weight, and where permitted development rights are usually restricted.
03
Locally listed &
non-designated heritage assets

Buildings identified by the local authority as locally important without being nationally listed. They do not need Listed Building Consent, but their significance is a material planning consideration.
04
Buildings affecting a
heritage setting

Properties that are not designated themselves but sit close enough to a listed building or historic landscape that the proposal is judged on its effect on that setting.

Extending a listed building

A listed building extension isn't prohibited. It's assessed. The conservation officer asks one question: does the addition harm what makes the building significant? That's a case you build with evidence.

The schemes that get consent share a pattern. They attach to the least significant part of the building, usually a later addition rather than the historic core. They stay subordinate in scale. They use a tight palette chosen to sit alongside the original, not imitate it. And they're reversible.

A contemporary extension is often the easier argument, not the harder one. Modern work reads as new work. National policy and most conservation officers prefer that to convincing pastiche.

Listed building
renovation & repair

Most listed building renovation projects are solving the same problem. The building has to work as a modern home, and what makes it worth protecting has to survive that. Layout, light, insulation, heating, bathrooms, kitchens: none of it was designed for.

So we start with what actually carries significance, because that's what tells you where you have room. Later partitions, twentieth-century alterations and bad previous work usually give far more latitude than owners expect. The fabric that matters gets repaired and specified properly, not replaced.

Work this
typically includes

Reordering internal layouts and reinstating lost proportions

Repair and restoration of historic fabric, joinery and elevations

Sensitive introduction of insulation, services, heating and glazing

Conversion or repurposing of redundant historic buildings and outbuildings

Conservation specifications and detailed drawings for the contractor

Complete renovation of period properties within conservation areas

Where a property is a period home but is not listed and not in a conservation area, the constraints are different and the project usually belongs with our luxury architecture service instead.

Listed Building Consent applications

Work that affects the character of a listed building needs Listed Building Consent, and most schemes need planning permission as well. The two are separate applications with different tests, and they are usually best submitted together so the authority assesses one coherent proposal rather than two partial ones.

Consent decisions turn on evidence. An application that explains what is significant about the building, what effect the proposal has on it and why that effect is acceptable is a different document from one that simply shows what is being built. Preparing that argument properly is the difference between a straightforward determination and a refusal.

If you want the regulations explained in detail before you speak to anyone, our Listed Building Consent guide covers what needs consent, what does not, and how retrospective applications are handled.

What we prepare and coordinate

  • Measured building surveys and existing-condition drawings
  • Feasibility advice and early engagement with the conservation officer
  • Pre-application submissions where they are likely to save time
  • Planning and Listed Building Consent applications and drawings
  • Heritage Statements, prepared through a specialist heritage consultant where required
  • Coordination of structural, ecology, landscape and other consultants
  • Discharge of planning and heritage conditions after consent

FAQs

Questions Before You Start

  • Do I need a listed building architect?

    Most people come to us wanting someone who can design the scheme and prepare the consent application to a standard the conservation officer will accept. That is the service we provide. Where a project requires an accredited conservation professional, a structural engineer with historic buildings experience or a specialist heritage consultant, we bring them into the team and coordinate their input.

  • Do I need Listed Building Consent for internal alterations?

    Often, yes. A listing covers the interior as well as the exterior, so removing a partition, altering a staircase, changing joinery or opening up a fireplace can all require consent even though none of it is visible from outside. Redecoration and like-for-like repair usually do not. The test is whether the work affects the character of the building as a building of special interest, and it is worth checking before work starts rather than after.

  • Do I need planning permission as well as Listed Building Consent?

    Usually. They are separate applications judged against different tests, and one does not substitute for the other. Extensions, outbuildings and most external changes need both. We prepare and submit them together so the authority is assessing a single coherent proposal.

  • Can I extend a Grade II listed building?

    Yes, subject to consent. Grade II listing does not prevent extension. What matters is where the extension attaches, how large it is relative to the original building, what it is built from and how convincingly the case is made that the significance of the building is not harmed. Extensions to later or less significant parts of a property are considerably easier to argue than additions to the historic core.

  • Can a listed building have a contemporary extension?

    Yes, and a contemporary approach is frequently better received than a historic imitation. National policy and most conservation officers prefer new work that is honestly of its own time and clearly distinguishable from the original, because it keeps the history of the building readable. The requirement is quality and restraint in the detailing, not the use of period materials.

  • What is a Heritage Statement and do I need one?

    A Heritage Statement sets out what is significant about the building and its setting, and assesses the effect the proposal would have on that significance. Any application affecting a designated heritage asset needs one, and its depth should be proportionate to the importance of the building and the scale of the work. We coordinate its preparation through a specialist heritage consultant and make sure the design and the statement say the same thing.

  • What is a non-designated heritage asset?

    It is a building or structure identified by the local authority as locally important, often through a local list, without being nationally listed. Listed Building Consent does not apply, but the significance of the asset is a material consideration in any planning decision affecting it, so the application still needs to address it properly.

  • What if previous owners carried out work without consent?

    This is more common than people expect and it is usually resolvable. The options are typically a retrospective application to regularise the work, a proposal that addresses the unauthorised alterations as part of a wider scheme, or reversal where the work has caused harm. It is worth dealing with, because unresolved unauthorised work will surface during a sale and can complicate any future application.

  • How much does a listed building specialist cost?

    Fees depend on the scale and complexity of the work, the surveys the building needs and how many specialist consultants have to be involved, so there is no single figure that would be honest. What we can do at the first conversation is give you a clear view of the stages your project needs, which of them we would carry out and what a realistic fee range looks like for a project of that type, before you commit to anything.
